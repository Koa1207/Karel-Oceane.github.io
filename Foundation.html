Chapter One

The market starts with a structural imbalance

Before we examine a single brand or retailer, the data reveals a gap that no stocking decision can fully close. The beauty industry has built its foundation shade market around lighter skin tones.

The remaining 79.7% serves lighter skin tones. Fair and Light alone account for 53% of the total market. This imbalance is structural — it exists before any brand or retailer decision is made, and no amount of stocking optimisation can compensate for it.

Shade depth in this analysis is determined using CIE L* lightnessMeasured objectively from product HEX codes derived from product HEX codes — not brand-provided shade names. This matters because naming conventions are not standardised across the industry. One brand's "deep" can be another brand's "medium."

By measuring the actual colour lightness of every shade, we can compare across brands and retailers on a consistent, objective scale. Five depth bands are defined by fixed L* thresholds: Very Deep below 35, Deep 35–45, Medium 45–55, Light 55–72, and Fair at 72 and above.

The distribution is not random. It reflects decades of product development decisions that prioritised lighter skin tones as the primary market. Deep and Very Deep shades were added incrementally — often as an afterthought — rather than designed as a core part of the range.

This is the baseline challenge. Any brand or retailer that wants to serve consumers with deeper skin tones is working against a market structure that was not built with them in mind.

Chapter Two

Not all brands are equally culpable

Some brands have made deliberate choices to serve deeper skin tones. But measuring that commitment requires more than a single percentage — and this is where most analysis goes wrong.

A brand can appear highly inclusive simply by having a small, entirely deep-shade range. The number that matters is not the percentage alone — it is the percentage in combination with the total range.

Foundation Accessibility Analysis · 2026

Marc Jacobs Beauty and Black Up both score 100% on deep shade coverage — every single shade they sell is classified as Deep or Very Deep. That sounds extraordinary. But both brands have very small total ranges. Serving a consumer with a deep skin tone who wants variety, different finishes, or different formulations? A 100% score with 10 shades serves them far less well than a 50% score with 150 shades.

This is why deep shade percentage alone is a misleading metric. The analysis requires two dimensions simultaneously: how committed is a brand to deeper tones as a proportion of its range, and how large is that range in absolute terms. Only brands that score highly on both dimensions offer genuinely accessible shade selection at scale.

Introducing the Inclusivity Score

To account for both dimensions simultaneously, an Inclusivity Score was developed — a composite of normalised deep shade percentage and normalised total shade count, scaled from 0 to 20. This score penalises brands with small deep-only ranges and rewards brands that combine genuine deep shade commitment with meaningful variety. A brand must do both to score highly.

Chapter Three

Sephora vs Ulta — the headline is misleading

At first glance, Sephora appears more inclusive. But the headline number hides a more complicated story — and a plot twist that changes the conclusion entirely.

The 1.3 percentage point gap between Sephora and Ulta looks like a clear result. But two complications immediately arise. First, Ulta actually carries a higher share of Very Deep shades — 5.0% versus Sephora's 4.9%. The deepest tones, which are the hardest to find, are slightly better represented at Ulta.

Second — and more significantly — Ulta carries a higher proportion of uncategorised shades at 11.4% compared to Sephora's 8.1%. These shades could not be depth-classified from their HEX codes, which means Ulta's true deep coverage may be understated. The headline gap is partly a measurement artefact.

When you look at the same brand at both stores, the picture reverses

The real test is not comparing retailer averages — it is comparing the same brand's deep shade coverage at each retailer. When brands stocked at both Sephora and Ulta are isolated, several demonstrate significantly stronger deep shade coverage at Ulta. Too Faced offers 8.2 percentage points more deep coverage at Ulta. MAC offers 6.5 percentage points more. Bare Minerals 5.5 points more. The retailer average obscures these brand-level differences entirely. The conclusion is clear: retailer averages can mislead. Brand stocking decisions are the true driver of access.

Conclusion

Three findings that change how you should shop

The data leads to three conclusions — one about the industry, one about retailers, and one that is directly actionable for any consumer with a deeper skin tone.

01
The inclusivity gap is structural
Deep and very deep shades account for only 20.3% of the market. No retailer or brand can fully compensate for this. Meaningful change requires brands to redesign their shade architecture from the ground up — not optimise their listings.
02
Brand choice drives access more than store choice
Sephora and Ulta look nearly equal at headline level. But for specific brands, one retailer can offer 5–8 percentage points more deep coverage. Choose by brand first, then check which store stocks the deeper shades.
03
Prioritise Inclusivity Score over retailer
BLKOPL, NYX, KVD Vegan Beauty, Bare Minerals and Morphe lead on both depth and range. Brands with a high inclusivity score maximise your chance of a match regardless of which store you shop in.

Limitations

  1. Shade depth in the lightness distribution is derived from colour lightness (L*) values based on HEX codes, while other charts rely on brand-provided shade categories.
  2. Uncategorised shades — particularly at Ulta — may influence distribution comparisons and could understate Ulta's true deep coverage.
  3. Availability assumptions are based on listed inventory and do not account for stock fluctuations.
